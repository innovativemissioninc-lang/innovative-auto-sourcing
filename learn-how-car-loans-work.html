FINANCING & LOANS

How Car Loans Actually Work: Rate, Term, and Total Cost

Three numbers decide what a car actually costs you: the price, the interest rate, and the term. Here's how they interact -- and why a lower monthly payment is often the more expensive choice.

Every car loan is built from three moving parts: the amount you borrow (the vehicle price minus your down payment and trade-in equity), the interest rate you're charged, and the term -- how many months you have to pay it back. Dealers and lenders can move any one of these to make a deal "work" on paper, and the easiest lever to pull is the term, because stretching it out lowers the monthly payment without lowering what you actually pay overall.

The monthly payment is the wrong number to shop by

A shorter loan at a slightly higher rate can cost you thousands less than a longer loan at a lower rate, because interest accrues on the outstanding balance for every month that balance exists. The number that actually tells you what a loan costs is the total cost of borrowing -- principal plus every dollar of interest paid over the full term -- not the payment amount a salesperson writes on a whiteboard.

What rates actually look like right now

As of 2026, the national average car loan rate in Canada sits in the mid-6% range, but the rate you'll actually be offered depends heavily on your credit tier: roughly 3.99%–6.99% for super-prime credit (720+), 5.99%–9.99% for prime (670–719), 8.99%–14.99% for near-prime (620–669), and 10.99%–29.99%+ for subprime (below 620). Used-vehicle loans typically carry higher rates than new, often 8% or more. The gap between a prime and a subprime rate on the same loan amount and term can easily mean a difference of several thousand dollars in total interest.

Term length changes the math more than people expect

The average new-vehicle loan in Canada is now around 72 months, and loans of 84 months or longer have grown sharply as a share of new-vehicle financing in recent years. A longer term lowers your monthly payment, but it also means you're paying interest for a lot more months, and your loan balance falls more slowly relative to the vehicle's depreciation -- which is the root cause of being upside-down (see our guide on what it means to be upside-down on a car loan).

What to actually check before you sign

  • APR, not just "rate" -- APR bakes in most lender fees, giving you a truer cost comparison between offers.
  • Total interest paid -- ask the lender or dealer to show this number in dollars, not just the monthly payment.
  • Prepayment terms -- some loans charge a penalty if you pay it off early, which matters if you plan to sell or trade in ahead of schedule.
  • Whether the rate is fixed for the full term -- most Canadian auto loans are fixed-rate, but confirm it in writing.

None of this requires financial expertise -- it just requires asking for the total-cost number instead of accepting the monthly payment as the whole story.
