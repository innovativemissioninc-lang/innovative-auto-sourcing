Why Used Vehicle Prices Went Up (and What's Changing)

The used car price surge of the early 2020s wasn't random -- it had specific causes, most of which have since eased. Here's the short version.

If you've noticed used vehicle prices feel higher than they "should" be compared to a few years ago, you're not imagining it -- but the specific causes behind that increase are mostly identifiable, and several have already started to ease.

What drove prices up

Global semiconductor shortages in the early 2020s cut new-vehicle production, which meant fewer new cars reaching dealer lots. That pushed buyers who couldn't get a new vehicle into the used market instead, driving used prices up sharply as demand outpaced a shrinking supply of available used inventory. Rental and fleet companies, which normally feed a steady stream of used vehicles back into the market after a few years, also held onto their fleets longer during this period, further tightening used supply.

What's eased since then

New-vehicle production has largely normalized, which has slowly rebuilt the pipeline of trade-ins and off-lease vehicles feeding the used market. Reporting through 2026 shows used-vehicle price inflation has come to an end nationally, with several provinces seeing prices flatten or ease slightly from their peaks.

What hasn't eased

New-vehicle prices themselves remain elevated -- averaging well over $60,000 in recent Canadian reporting -- which keeps a floor under used prices, since a used vehicle's value is always relative to what a comparable new one costs. Interest rates also remain meaningfully higher than the ultra-low-rate years before 2022, which affects what buyers can afford on a monthly basis even when sticker prices themselves aren't rising further.

What this means practically

  • The sharpest run-up in used prices is behind us, but "back to pre-2020 pricing" isn't a realistic expectation.
  • Financing cost is now a bigger share of what determines affordability than it was a few years ago -- rate matters as much as price.
  • A cooling market rewards buyers who compare real, current listings rather than relying on outdated price expectations from a few years back.
